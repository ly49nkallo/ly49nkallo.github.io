To Use AI Tools Ethically as a Programmer and Scientist -- My Experience

August the 3rd, 2026

I have been thinking a lot about this issue--perhaps it is the issue of our time: how the f*ck do we live alongside a machine that is smarter than us?

Talking with many different people, and after personal reflection on my own behaviors, I think I can share an idea that can act as a preliminary answer to the above question...

Coming from the perspective of a person who does most of their "work" in the setting of physics research labs, the question of *how automated* is optimal has been haunting me for a while.

I work on the top floor of the Berkeley Physics building where the very best theoretical physicists also work. Walking past their offices, I can observe that they converse (seemingly routinely) with the robot.

My mom, who is a PI and Professor at a large research lab at UCLA routinely sings the praises of Claude For Science (a claude wrapper specifically designed for the area of science she works in)

Work that she would previously delegate to undergrads such as data analysis and plot making she can now do in 10 minutes on her own. She often tells me she has replicated the work of what took grad students weeks in one session with CFS.

Some of her undergrads she keeps around for their technical skills she is getting upset at because they keep running into computer issues that are easily solved by the Robot in one session. What she tells them is to overcome their issues by using Claude themselves. She says she tells them "I could do it with Claude in 10 minutes, so you should be able to as well". When the undergrads asks to see what prompts she used to do their whole project, she says "figure it out yourself".

However, based on my own experience, there is a danger to this, just as anything taken to the extreme becomes toxic: Claude is smarter than an undergrad, so Claude can steal the deep and detaiiled understanding that usually comes from implementation from an undergrad by taking on too many responsibilities and introducing assumptions without consulting the user first.

My theory is this: throughout history, teams have been made of two kinds of people: those who have deep understanding but perhaps little technical implementation knowledge (aka most Professors) and those with abundant technical acumen but little understanding of the field (undergrads)

This is because knowledge of the field requires decades of constant exposure and participation, something impossible for even the most motivated undergrads, while technical skills requires growing up with them and the ability to learn bleeding-edge technology quickly and deeply (a skill expressed by many undergrads who grew up learning to code, for example)

Perhaps the "Professor" type (project leads, CEOs, Lead software engineers) are very/somewhat knowledgeable in implementation/techincal details, but it would be an inefficient use of their time to do it themselves. Either way, the two distinct classes exist and remain distinct.

With the introduction of powerful and effective AI tools, the need for the "undergrad class" of people has greatly diminished. Instead, they should be promoted to the "professor class" of people by rigorously enforcing that they simulaneously use AI for non-field related, labourious tasks, but retain and refine a careful and detailed understanding of the overall theory to best prepare them to become the "professor class" of people.

This model even extends to fields such as software engineering and computer science/programming and web development. The role of expert overview and implementer has always been blurred for these fields but the distinction is still important and the previous advice is still applicable, perhaps even more so, due to a increased level of effectiveness.
